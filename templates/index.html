What this model does

It answers one question: “Will this customer pay their next credit-card bill?” It reads six months of a customer’s financial behaviour and returns a probability of default between 0 and 1.

Because the outcome is a yes/no event, this is a binary classification problem. The probability is what actually matters to a lender: it lets them move the approval cut-off up or down depending on how much risk they are willing to carry.

Target: default = 1 means the customer failed to pay their October 2005 bill. All predictors describe April–September 2005, so no feature can see the future.

How a prediction is made

01Raw application23 fields: limit, demographics, 6 months of repayment status, bills and payments
02Feature engineering29 credit-risk features derived row-wise: utilisation, repayment effort, delinquency counts
03PreprocessingMedian imputation and one-hot encoding, fitted on training data only
04{{ model_name }}Outputs a probability of default
05DecisionCompared against the tuned {{ '%.3f'|format(threshold) }} threshold → approve or decline

Steps 02–04 are stored together in models/final_model.pkl, so raw applicant data can be scored directly.

Why accuracy is the wrong headline metric

The accuracy trap. A model that simply predicts “nobody defaults” scores 77.87% accuracy — higher than three of the four real models — while catching zero defaulters. That is why this project is judged on ROC-AUC, recall and PR-AUC instead.

The two mistakes cost different amounts

  • False negative — predicted repay, actually defaulted. A bad loan was approved; the bank loses the principal. Expensive.
  • False positive — predicted default, actually repaid. A good customer was declined; the bank loses the profit margin. Cheaper.

So the threshold was tuned for recall

Total cost was defined as {{ cost_fn|int }} × FN + {{ cost_fp|int }} × FP and minimised on out-of-fold training predictions — never on the test set. That moved the cut-off from 0.500 to {{ '%.3f'|format(threshold) }}, lifting recall from {{ '%.4f'|format(metrics_at_half.get('Recall', 0)) }} to {{ '%.4f'|format(metrics.get('Recall', 0)) }}.

    The dataset

    UCI “Default of Credit Card Clients” — 30,000 real customers of a Taiwanese bank, April–September 2005.

    • 29,965 rows after removing 35 exact duplicates.
    • 22.13% default rate — imbalanced at 3.52 : 1.
    • No missing values in the source data.
    • ID dropped — a row identifier carries no signal and would let a tree memorise individual customers.
    • Undocumented category codes (EDUCATION 0/5/6, MARRIAGE 0) folded into others.

    Why this dataset and not German Credit?

    The better-known Statlog German Credit set has only 1,000 rows and no payment time series. The assignment requires feature engineering from financial history, which is impossible without one. This dataset provides six months of repayment status, bills and payments for 30,000 customers.

    What financial factors are most associated with credit risk?

    Payment behaviour dominates, not demographics. How often someone has been late and whether they are late right now outweigh every other feature combined. engineered marks features that do not exist in the raw data.

    False negatives are “clean-record” defaulters

    The defaulters the model misses average 0.08 late months — essentially spotless — and were paying on time right up to the month they defaulted.

    The model cannot see a sudden shock — job loss, illness, a business failure — because nothing in six months of card history announces it. That is a limit of the data, not a fixable modelling bug.

    Their mean predicted probability is 0.294 against a {{ '%.3f'|format(threshold) }} threshold: the model was uncertain, not confidently wrong.

    Limitations you should state out loud

    • One market, one period. Taiwan, 2005. Behaviour and economic conditions differ elsewhere and today.
    • Six-month window only. No income, employment, or other-lender data that real credit bureaus use.
    • Card default, not bankruptcy. The target is one missed payment.
    • The {{ cost_fn|int }}:{{ cost_fp|int }} cost ratio is an assumption. A real lender would use its own loss-given-default figures.
    • Precision {{ '%.4f'|format(metrics.get('Precision', 0)) }}. Suited to a review-and-escalate workflow, not fully automated rejection.
    • Selection bias. The data only contains customers who were already approved for a card.
    • No calibration analysis. Probabilities are used for ranking, not verified to be literally accurate.
